College Education Analysis Vignette

DATA-613: Jon Cote

DATA-413: Camden Egan, Jack Markert, John Dye, Shae Ramberg

College Education Analysis is an interactive website built with Shiny that helps students, families, and college staff explore information about four‑year colleges in the United States. It lets people look at costs, student debt, earnings after graduation, and graduation rates so they can find good‑value schools and study what makes students more likely to finish college.

Part 1: Use Case

For students and families, the main goal is to find colleges that give strong results for a reasonable cost. On the Student tab, users can choose filters such as region, state, public or private, and how urban or rural the campus is, then see numbers like tuition, typical student debt, and average earnings 10 years after graduation, along with a Best Value Index that compares earnings to the combined net cost and debt. They can mark specific schools as favorites so those schools stand out in tables and graphs, and they can use an interactive map to look for schools in certain locations while seeing each school’s Best Value Index and other details when they hover over a point.

For college researchers, administrators, and policy makers, the tool is meant to help them understand how school characteristics are linked to student success. On the Researcher tab, they can first look at one variable at a time, such as faculty salary or net price, to see how it is spread across schools in a region. Then they can move to tools that show how two or more variables are related and run simple statistical tests to compare groups. In the modeling section, they can set graduation rate as the outcome and choose predictors like tuition, admission rate, average faculty salary, student demographics, debt, and earnings to fit regression models and see the results in clear tables and charts that support planning and policy choices.

Part 2: Required Packages

The app uses Shiny to run the interactive website, with buttons, sliders, and tabs that respond to user choices in real time. It uses readr, dplyr, and other tidyverse tools to read in the College Scorecard data, create new measures like NET_COST_CALC and Best_Value_Index, rename columns to more understandable labels, and build lists of options for drop‑down menus. Graphs and interactive displays are created with ggplot2, DT for filterable and sortable data tables, and leaflet for an interactive map of U.S. colleges where users can hover over points to see Best Value Index and other values. The package gridExtra is used to arrange multiple ggplot figures on a single page (for example, stacking numeric and categorical plots together) so they can be shown as a combined display in the app. The overall look and feel of the app, including colors and styles for plots, is controlled with bslib and thematic so that everything has a consistent, easy‑to‑read design.

Part 3: Data Source and Structure

The default data source for this analysis is a consolidated dataset drawn from the U.S. Department of Education’s College Scorecard (2022-2023 academic year) and the Integrated Postsecondary Education Data System (IPEDS). These sources provide comprehensive institutional characteristics, graduation rates, and financial metrics. To ensure a focused and relevant analysis, we have filtered the raw data to include only 4-year, public or private non-profit institutions that are currently operating, predominantly bachelor’s-degree granting, and enroll more than 100 undergraduate students.

The general structure of the final processed data consists of a single data frame containing 1,865 observations (institutions) and 62 variables. The dataset includes key identifiers and location details such as the institution name (INSTNM), unit ID (unitid), city (CITY), state (STABBR), and geographic coordinates. It features categorical variables describing institutional control (Public vs. Private Non-profit) and special designations like Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCU) or Hispanic-Serving Institutions (HSI). The data also tracks quantitative student metrics, including demographics (UGDS_MEN, UGDS_WOMEN), academic performance (SAT_AVG, ADM_RATE), and completion rates (C150_4), alongside financial variables such as in-state and out-of-state tuition (TUITIONFEE_IN, TUITIONFEE_OUT), median graduate debt (GRAD_DEBT_MDN), and post-entry earnings (MD_EARN_WNE_P10).

Part 5: Statistical Analysis Inputs, Controls, and Outputs (Jon)

The Statistical Analysis tools of the College Education Analysis app are designed for researchers, administrators, and policy analysts who want to formally test relationships between institutional characteristics and student outcomes. These tools are located in the Researchers and Administrators tab and consist of three components: Single Variable Exploration, Multi-Variable Exploration, and Model Testing. This section focuses on how users apply the Multi-Variable Exploration and Model Testing tools to generate and interpret statistical results.

Statistical Analysis Scenario

A common research question that can be addressed using this section of the app is:

Do more selective and higher-value institutions tend to have higher graduation rates after accounting for earnings and cost?

To answer this, the researcher first explores bivariate relationships using the Multi-Variable Exploration tool and then fits a multiple linear regression model using the Model Testing tool.

Multi-Variable Analysis: Inputs and Controls

In the Multi-Variable Exploration subtab, users are given the following controls:

  • X Variable (mv_x): Numeric predictor selected from numeric_vars

  • Y Variable (mv_y): Numeric outcome selected from numeric_vars

  • Grouping Variable (mv_group): Optional categorical grouping

  • Control (Public vs Private)

  • Region

  • HBCU status

  • Region Filter (mv_region): Restricts analysis to one region or all regions

For this scenario, the researcher selects:

  • X Variable = Admission Rate

  • Y Variable = Graduation Rate

  • Grouping Variable = Control (Public vs Private)

  • Region = All regions

Once these inputs are selected, the app removes observations with missing data and generates a scatterplot, a grouped boxplot, and a formal statistical test.

Model Testing: Inputs and Controls

After exploring pairwise relationships, the researcher moves to the Model Testing subtab to fit a multiple linear regression model predicting graduation rate. In this section, the response variable is fixed as Graduation Rate (C150_4). The user selects predictors from numeric_vars and applies optional filters.

Available controls include:

  • Predictor Variables (mt_predictors): Multiple numeric predictors

  • Region Filter (mt_region)

  • School Type (mt_school_type)

  • Fit Regression Model Button (mt_fit)

Statistical Outputs and Interpretation

The Model Testing subtab produces two coordinated outputs:

  • Model Summary Output Displays coefficient estimates, standard errors, t-statistics, p-values, R-squared, adjusted R-squared, and the overall F-statistic.

  • Coefficient Table Output A formatted table of regression coefficients for interpretation.

Figure ?@fig-mt-coefs summarizes the estimated effect of each predictor on graduation rate while holding all other predictors constant. Interpretation of the model follows standard regression logic:

  • A negative coefficient on Admission Rate indicates that more selective institutions tend to have higher graduation rates.

  • A positive coefficient on Median Earnings After 10 Years suggests that institutions whose graduates earn more also tend to exhibit higher completion rates.

  • A positive coefficient on Best Value Index indicates that institutions providing stronger earnings relative to cost and debt show improved student success.

  • The Adjusted R-squared quantifies how much of the variation in graduation rates is explained by the selected predictors.

By adjusting predictors, regions, and school type filters, researchers can compare how institutional drivers of graduation outcomes vary across geographic and sector contexts. This enables the app to support evidence-based institutional planning, benchmarking, and higher-education policy analysis.

Part 6: References

Below are our 3 relevant literature reviews with full citations:

Review 1:

 Loeb, Susanna, and Marianne E. Page. “Examining the Link Between Teacher Wages and Student Outcomes: The Importance of Alternative Labor Market Opportunities and Non-Pecuniary Variation.” The Review of Economics and Statistics, vol. 82, no. 3, Aug. 2000, pp. 393-408. Stanford CEPA, https://cepa.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/loebpage.pdf.

The relationship between university resources and student success is complex, as studies often fail to find a consistent link between per-pupil spending and better outcomes. A study by Susanna Loeb and Marianne E. Page argues this is because previous research overlooked key variables, especially alternative labor market opportunities. They found that a high salary alone doesn’t attract the best educators if other, more lucrative professions are available. By creating a “relative wage” measure that compares teacher salaries to those of other local professionals, Loeb and Page isolated the true effect of compensation. After controlling for external factors, they found a 10% increase in relative teacher wages reduces high school dropout rates by 3% to 4%. This demonstrates that competitive salaries do positively impact student retention. The Loeb & Page study provides a strong theoretical justification for our new problem for researchers when looking at how factors, specifically faculty salary in this case, is a predictor for graduation rates. While their study focused on K-12, its core principle is identical: investing in instructional quality (via competitive salaries) is directly linked to positive student outcomes. This review supports the hypothesis that a university’s investment in faculty can have a measurable, positive association with student success, justifying its inclusion as a variable in our regression model. 

Review 2:

Oreopoulos, Philip, and Uros Petronijevic. “Making college worth it: A review of research on the returns to Higher Education.” The Future of Children, vol. 23, no. 1, May 2013, pp. 41–65, https://doi.org/10.3386/w19053. 

Making College Worth It: A Review of the Returns to Higher Education” by Philip Oreopoulos and Uros Petronijevic synthesizes research on the economic value of higher education. Although the authors affirm that college generally provides strong labor market returns, they emphasize that outcomes vary by student characteristics, academic pathways, and institutional quality. A central conclusion is that the earnings payoff to college remains large. Observational studies estimate that each year of four-year college raises annual earnings by 6–9%. However, the returns are not uniform. Major choice, occupation, and institutional selectivity significantly shape long-term earnings. While some studies detect limited differences across college quality, the broader literature indicates that more selective institutions tend to produce higher earnings. This question of the effects of selectivity and price on future earnings is key to our project as seen in our Best Value Index. Key variables across the reviewed studies include years of schooling, degree attainment, field of study, institutional selectivity, demographics, and earnings. The data sources span administrative state university records, national surveys, census data, and field experiments.

Review 3:

Hemelt, Steven W., and Dave E. Marcotte. “The Impact of Tuition Increases on Enrollment at Public Colleges and Universities.” Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis, vol. 33, no. 4, Dec. 2011, pp. 435–457. American Educational Research Association. https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373711415261

The study by Steven W. Hemelt and Dave E. Marcotte (2011) provides foundational insight into how tuition costs affect enrollment at public universities. Using IPEDS data, they found that a $100 tuition increase leads to a ~0.25% enrollment decline, an effect most pronounced at Research I universities. The study also highlights that financial aid, especially Pell Grants, can mitigate these negative effects; Pell funding had a stronger positive impact on enrollment than tuition had a negative one. However, Hemelt and Marcotte note that lower-income students and those at less selective institutions are more sensitive to these financial changes. This research directly informs our project, as it provides an empirical basis for our hypothesis: if declining aid relative to tuition lowers enrollment, it will also negatively impact graduation rates. Their findings reinforce the importance of tracking the aid-to-tuition ratio over time, especially when analyzing institutional differences and demographic inequities.
